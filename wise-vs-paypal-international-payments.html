How freelancers and creators get paid by international clients (2026): Wise vs PayPal fees compared

Research-based comparison. Sources checked November 2, 2026 (US fee pages). Not hands-on tested. Fees change, so check current pricing on wise.com and paypal.com before you decide.

If you freelance, sell services, or get paid by sponsors and clients outside your country, two tools come up again and again: PayPal and Wise. They solve the problem in different ways. PayPal works like a checkout: the client pays you through PayPal, and PayPal takes a percentage. Wise gives you account details (for example, US routing and account numbers, or a EUR IBAN), so the client pays you by ordinary bank transfer. You then convert or withdraw the money.

This guide compares what each company publishes on its own US fee pages. We didn't run test payments for this article. Every figure below comes from an official page linked in the Sources section. Where we do arithmetic, it's an illustration built from those published rates, not a quote.

The short answer

  • Wise is usually cheaper when a client can pay by bank transfer. Wise lists receiving domestic (non-wire, non-Swift) payments in AUD, CAD, EUR, GBP, HUF, NZD, PHP, SGD and USD as free, and its currency conversion fee starts from 0.23%, varying by currency.
  • PayPal is usually more convenient when a client wants to pay by card or PayPal balance, wants a one-click invoice, or is a platform that only pays out through PayPal. That convenience costs a percentage: for an international commercial payment, PayPal adds 1.50% on top of its domestic rate, plus a fixed fee.
  • Many freelancers use both: Wise account details for clients who can do a bank transfer, and PayPal for those who can't.

How each one works when a client pays you

Wise: share account details, get paid "like a local"

With Wise you open account details in a currency and share them with your client. Wise's receiving page says you can "get paid like a local" in AUD, CAD, EUR, GBP, HUF, NZD, PHP, SGD and USD without fees. In practice, a client in Germany can pay your EUR details by an ordinary SEPA transfer, and a US client can pay your USD details by ACH. To them it's a normal domestic bank payment.

Once the money arrives, it sits in that currency in your Wise account. Holding money is listed as free. You can convert it to another currency (from 0.23%, varying by currency), spend it with the Wise card, or send it to your regular bank.

A few details matter:

  • Wires and Swift aren't free. If your client sends a USD wire or an international Swift payment, Wise lists a fixed fee per payment (6.11 USD for USD wire/Swift, 2.16 GBP for GBP Swift, 2.39 EUR for EUR Swift). Ask clients to use the local transfer option where they can. Wise says "there'll always be a free alternative."
  • Business accounts pay a one-time set-up fee. Wise's business pricing page lists account details at 31 USD, described as one set-up fee that covers every Wise Business feature. Personal account details are listed as free.
  • Limits apply to some currencies. Wise says most currencies have no receiving limits, "with the exception of USD and SGD." Check the limits page if you receive large USD amounts.
  • Wise is not a bank. Wise explains that it safeguards customer money (keeps it separate and holds it in cash or secure liquid assets) instead of lending it out, which is different from bank deposit insurance.

PayPal: the client pays through PayPal, PayPal takes a percentage

With PayPal, the client pays from their PayPal account, by card, or through a PayPal invoice. For US business accounts, PayPal's merchant fee page lists 3.49% + a fixed fee for PayPal Checkout, 2.99% + a fixed fee for standard card payments, and 2.99% for "Send/Receive Money for Goods and Services." When the payer is in a different country, PayPal says the domestic fee applies plus 1.50% for international commercial transactions. The fixed fee depends on the currency you receive: 0.49 USD, 0.39 EUR or 0.39 GBP, for example.

Invoices follow the same pattern. Invoice payments by PayPal are 3.49% + fixed fee, card payments 2.99% + fixed fee, and international invoice payments add 1.50%. PayPal also offers an optional invoicing subscription at 14.99 USD per month.

Then there's currency conversion. If PayPal converts money for you, the exchange rate includes a spread: 3.00% for most transactions, or 4.00% for some payment types (or another amount disclosed during the transaction). Moving money to your linked bank by standard transfer is free when no conversion is involved.

One caution: PayPal's "Friends and Family" personal payments are defined as payments that are not for goods or services. Client work is a commercial transaction, so expect the commercial rates above.

A worked illustration: a $1,000 invoice from an overseas client

This is arithmetic using the published US rates above, not a test transaction. Your real cost depends on how the client pays, your country and any rate shown at checkout.

  • PayPal, client pays with their PayPal account: 3.49% + 1.50% international = 4.99% of $1,000 = $49.90, plus the 0.49 USD fixed fee = about $50.39. You'd receive about $949.61 in USD.
  • PayPal, client pays by card through your invoice: 2.99% + 1.50% = 4.49% = $44.90, plus 0.49 USD = about $45.39.
  • Wise, client pays your USD account details by local (ACH) transfer: listed as free to receive. If they send a USD wire or Swift payment instead, the listed fee is 6.11 USD.
  • Wise, client in the eurozone pays your EUR details by SEPA: free to receive. If you then convert the euros to dollars, the conversion fee starts from 0.23% and varies by currency, so check the Wise calculator for the exact figure on the day.

The gap grows with invoice size, because PayPal's fees are mostly percentages and Wise's local receiving is free. On a small one-off payment, the gap is a few dollars. Convenience may matter more than fees then.

Where PayPal is the better fit

Being fair, Wise doesn't win every time:

  • Your client can only pay by card or PayPal. Wise account details take bank transfers. If a client won't or can't do a bank transfer, PayPal (or a card processor) is the practical choice.
  • Platforms that pay out by PayPal. Many marketplaces, affiliate programs and creator platforms pay through PayPal. If that's how the money comes in, you need a PayPal account regardless.
  • Checkout and invoicing in one place. PayPal invoices let clients pay in a few clicks with their PayPal balance, card or Pay Later options. That can mean faster payment from clients who don't want to set up a bank transfer.
  • Swift-only clients and small amounts. If a client can only send an international Swift wire, Wise charges a fixed receiving fee. On a very small payment, that fixed fee can be a meaningful share of the total.

Where Wise is the better fit

  • You invoice regularly in USD, EUR, GBP, CAD, AUD or another supported currency, and your clients can pay by local bank transfer.
  • You want to hold several currencies and convert when it suits you, at a published fee instead of a 3–4% spread.
  • You're a business that can justify the one-time 31 USD business set-up fee against ongoing savings.

  1. Open a Wise account, then get account details for the currencies your clients pay in. Business accounts have the one-time fee above. Open a Wise account (this is a referral link).
